# Vision

## 1. Meaning

**Vision is the activity, pursuit, or action a person wants to undertake.** It describes what the person wants to do, explore, build, create, understand, solve, change, or experience.

Vision is about intended action—not a fixed occupational identity. It may be concrete, broad, emerging, or currently unclear.

Examples:
- “I want to build machines that can perform tasks people find difficult.”
- “I want to investigate how people learn.”
- “I want to create stories through animation.”
- “I want to solve problems in how people access healthcare.”

A vision can be small or ambitious, immediate or long-term, practical or exploratory.

## 2. What Vision Is Not

### Not an occupational title
“I want to be a software engineer” names a socially recognized role. It may point toward a vision, but it does not explain what the person actually wants to do within or beyond that role.

The same title can represent very different activities. A software engineer might want to build educational tools, study algorithms, automate business processes, or create a company.

### Not the same as impact
“I want to build an app” describes an activity. “I want students in rural communities to access learning materials” describes a desired outcome. These may be connected, but they are distinct.

### Not the same as interest
Finding robotics fascinating is an attraction. Designing or building robots is a possible vision. Interest may support a vision without amounting to an intention to pursue it.

### Not a prediction or permanent commitment
A vision is a current expression of intended activity, not a promise about what someone will do forever. It can evolve through exposure, experience, and experimentation.

### Not proof of ability or feasibility
Wanting to build a medical device does not establish that the person currently has the technical knowledge, resources, or authorization needed to do so.

## 3. How Vision Is Recognized

Vision is most clearly represented by an action or pursuit. It may appear as:
- A thing the person wants to make, build, design, investigate, or improve.
- A problem or question they want to work on.
- An activity they imagine spending time doing.
- A process they want to participate in or lead.
- A type of experience they want to create or enable.

A useful distinction is between the person's chosen activity and the label they attach to it. A title can be a clue, but the underlying activity gives the vision its substance.

Vision may be:
- **Defined:** The intended activity is sufficiently clear.
- **Partially defined:** The person has fragments, examples, or a broad direction, but the activity is not yet specific.
- **Undefined:** The person cannot currently articulate an intended activity.
- **Contradictory:** Several stated pursuits do not yet fit together.
- **Insufficiently evidenced:** A claim exists, but its meaning or stability is unclear.

An undefined vision does not mean the person has no aspirations. It means the intended activity is not yet known or articulated.

## 4. Relationship to the Other Pillars

### Vision and Impact
Vision is what the person wants to do; impact is what they want that activity to accomplish. They often appear in one sentence but answer different questions.

Example: “I want to develop low-cost water sensors so communities can identify unsafe water.”
- Vision: develop low-cost water sensors.
- Impact: help communities identify unsafe water.

A person may know the activity but not its intended outcome, or know the outcome without knowing what activity could produce it.

### Vision and Interest
Interest is attraction; vision is intended pursuit. A person may be interested in astronomy without wanting to conduct astronomical research. Conversely, a person may pursue an activity for reasons other than enjoyment.

Interest can offer clues to possible visions, but it is not conclusive evidence of intention.

### Vision and Skill
Skill is current capacity; vision is intended activity. A person can want to do something they cannot yet do. The distance between vision and current skill may indicate a learning need, not an invalid vision.

Existing skills may also reveal activities the person has not yet considered.

### Vision and Resources
Resources are forms of leverage available or accessible to the person. They affect what can be attempted, when, and under what conditions; they do not define what the person wants to do.

A lack of current access may constrain or delay a vision without erasing it.

## 5. Key Distinction

**Vision describes intended action, not the name of the person who performs it.** A professional role may later become one possible way to express a vision, but the role is not the vision itself.
