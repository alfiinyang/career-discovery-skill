# Resources

## 1. Meaning

**Resources are the forms of capacity, access, support, or leverage a person can draw on to pursue an activity and contribute to an intended outcome.**

A resource does not have to be personally owned. It may be available through legitimate access, relationships, institutions, communities, or collaboration.

Resources include:
- **Personal:** time, energy, attention, financial means, mobility, and available capacity.
- **Skills and knowledge:** abilities, expertise, learning, and relevant understanding.
- **Material and technical:** devices, tools, software, equipment, workspace, labs, and infrastructure.
- **Social:** mentors, peers, collaborators, family support, professional relationships, and networks.
- **Institutional:** university facilities, school programs, employer support, libraries, communities, and organizational access.
- **Experiential:** projects, work history, volunteering, lived experience, and exposure to a field.
- **Credentials and reputation:** qualifications, references, demonstrated work, and credibility that enable access to opportunities.

## 2. What Resources Are Not

### Not just money
Financial capacity is one resource among many. A person may have limited funds but possess relevant skills, access to equipment, supportive relationships, time, or institutional opportunities.

### Not limited to what a person owns
A university lab, community workspace, mentor, open-source tool, or employer-provided system may be a resource if the person can legitimately access or leverage it.

### Not the same as skill
Skill is a person's capacity to perform an activity. Resources are broader forms of leverage. Skills can function as resources, but not every resource is a skill.

### Not automatically useful
A resource is relevant in relation to a particular vision or impact. An expensive camera may be valuable for a filmmaking pursuit but have little relevance to a person's current goal of studying network security.

### Not the same as opportunity or guaranteed access
A possible grant, job, mentor, or lab is not necessarily an available resource. Its accessibility, conditions, timing, and reliability matter.

### Not a complete inventory of everything in someone's life
The significance of a resource depends on what the person is trying to do and accomplish. Resources are contextual rather than an abstract list of possessions.

## 3. How Resources Are Recognized

A resource is understood through its relationship to a specific pursuit or outcome:
- What the person already has or can reliably access.
- Who or what can provide relevant support, knowledge, equipment, or entry.
- What prior experience, work, or relationships may be leveraged.
- What constraints affect availability, such as cost, time, location, permission, or competing responsibilities.
- What resource is missing, inaccessible, or only a possibility.
- Whether the resource is sufficient for the intended activity, and at what scale.

Resources may be:
- **Available:** Present or reliably accessible now.
- **Potentially accessible:** A plausible source exists, but access is not yet confirmed.
- **Constrained:** A resource exists but is limited by time, cost, permission, capacity, or other conditions.
- **Unavailable:** The person currently lacks access to the relevant resource.
- **Unidentified:** The person has not yet recognized or articulated relevant resources.
- **Insufficiently evidenced:** Availability or usefulness has been asserted but is not yet clear.

Not knowing of a resource does not prove that none exists. A resource may be hidden in the person's environment or outside their current awareness.

## 4. Relationship to the Other Pillars

### Resources and Vision
Vision describes intended activity; resources affect how, when, and under what conditions it can be pursued. Resources do not determine what a person wants, although constraints may shape the available routes.

A vision can remain meaningful even when the resources needed to pursue it are not yet available.

### Resources and Impact
Impact is the intended outcome. The resources needed depend on the nature and scale of that outcome. Creating a prototype, testing it with users, and deploying it widely may require different resources.

The desired impact helps establish which resources are relevant.

### Resources and Interest
Exposure and access can reveal or deepen interests. A person who has never encountered a robotics lab may not know whether they enjoy building robots.

Limited access can restrict opportunities to discover interests; it should not be mistaken for lack of interest.

### Resources and Skill
Skills are capacities a person can deploy and are therefore one kind of resource. Other resources—such as equipment, collaborators, time, and institutional access—can enable the use or development of skills.

Resources can also compensate for individual gaps through collaboration, instruction, or access to expertise, without implying that the person already possesses that expertise.

## 5. Key Distinction

**Resources are what a person can leverage—not only what they own or already know.** Their relevance and sufficiency can be understood only in relation to a particular vision and intended impact.
