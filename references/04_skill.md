# Skill

## 1. Meaning

**Skill is a person's current capacity to perform an activity or carry out a task.** It includes technical and nontechnical abilities, at any level of depth or proficiency.

A skill is relevant to this framework when the person can demonstrate or independently deploy some degree of the capacity—not merely express interest in learning it.

Examples:
- Writing and debugging a simple program.
- Explaining a concept clearly.
- Repairing a device.
- Planning an event.
- Analyzing data.
- Listening carefully, coordinating a group, or resolving a practical problem.

Skills may be developed through formal education, independent practice, work, caregiving, hobbies, community participation, or lived experience.

## 2. What Skill Is Not

### Not an interest
Wanting to learn Python is an interest or learning intention, not proof of Python skill.

### Not a credential
A certificate, course completion, job title, or degree can provide evidence of exposure or achievement, but does not automatically establish current ability to perform a specific task.

Conversely, a person may have real skills without formal credentials.

### Not merely a self-description
Statements such as “I'm good at leadership” or “I'm a programmer” are claims. Their meaning and strength depend on what the person can actually do and the evidence available.

Self-report is useful information, but it is distinct from demonstrated capacity.

### Not limited to technical abilities
Communication, observation, organization, teaching, negotiation, persistence in problem-solving, and practical judgment can all be skills when they represent deployable capacities.

### Not a measure of personal worth or fixed potential
A current skill gap describes what someone cannot yet reliably do. It does not establish an inability to learn or a limit on future development.

## 3. How Skill Is Recognized

Skill can be understood through:
- Tasks the person can complete independently or with a known level of support.
- Examples of work, projects, decisions, or problems they have handled.
- The complexity, consistency, and quality of their performance.
- Contexts in which the skill has been used.
- Feedback, outcomes, demonstrations, or other available evidence.
- The distinction between what the person can do now and what they are learning.

Evidence can vary in strength. A completed project or observed performance may provide more direct evidence than a broad self-description, while a credible account of past performance can still be informative.

Skill may be:
- **Defined:** A capacity and its evidence are sufficiently clear.
- **Partially defined:** Some ability is apparent, but level, independence, or scope is uncertain.
- **Undefined:** The person cannot currently identify or describe their abilities.
- **Contradictory:** Claims and examples, or different accounts of performance, do not reconcile.
- **Insufficiently evidenced:** A skill is claimed or suspected, but available evidence is not enough to establish it.

An undefined or insufficiently evidenced skill profile does not mean the person has no skills. It may indicate limited self-awareness, lack of opportunity to demonstrate ability, or missing evidence.

## 4. Relationship to the Other Pillars

### Skill and Vision
Vision is intended activity; skill is current capacity. A person may have the skills to do something they do not want to pursue, or want to pursue something for which they lack skills.

The gap between a vision and current skills may identify learning, practice, or collaboration needs.

### Skill and Impact
Impact is the desired outcome. Skills influence which outcomes a person can contribute to directly and what expertise or partners may be needed.

Ambitious impact does not have to be abandoned because current skills are insufficient; it may require a longer path or a team.

### Skill and Interest
Interest is attraction; skill is ability. A person can have high ability and low interest, or high interest and low ability. Neither relationship should be assumed from the other.

Their relationship can help distinguish an activity the person enjoys and can already perform from one they enjoy but need to learn.

### Skill and Resources
Skills are both capacities and resources. They can be leveraged directly, exchanged, taught, or combined with other resources.

However, the broader resource category includes more than skill: time, equipment, access, relationships, experience, credentials, and institutional support may also matter.

## 5. Key Distinction

**Skill is what the person can currently do, supported by evidence appropriate to the claim.** It is distinct from what they want to do, what attracts them, and what they hope to accomplish.
